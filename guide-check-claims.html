How to check a viral claim before sharing it.

Use a repeatable method to examine forwarded messages, headlines, screenshots, images and dramatic claims.

1. Pause before forwarding

Emotional wording, urgency and calls to forward something to everyone are reasons to pause. A familiar sender, large number of shares or professional-looking graphic does not establish that a claim is true.

2. Find the original source

Look for the original announcement, report, research paper, official notice or full video. A screenshot or a clipped quote often omits context. If a message claims a department has announced a new rule, find the department's own website or official channel.

3. Check the date and context

An old true story can be presented as a new event. A real photo may be from another place, another year or a different event. Search distinctive wording, examine the original publication date, and compare the claim with the surrounding context.

4. Confirm with independent sources

Look for reliable sources that provide evidence, not just repeated copies of the same post. For important claims, see whether reputable outlets or subject-matter organisations independently report the same facts. A lack of coverage does not automatically prove a claim false, but it is a reason for further checking.

5. Separate fact from interpretation

Ask: what can be directly verified, what is opinion, what is prediction, and what is not yet known? Headlines can be simplified or dramatic. Read beyond the headline and inspect any linked documents.

6. For claims about the Government of India

The official PIB Fact Check portal provides a way to submit or examine suspicious information concerning the Government of India. Its scope is not every scientific, health, local or personal claim, so use other suitable primary sources for those topics.

7. Check suspicious messages and links separately

Fact-checking a claim is different from checking whether a link is safe. Do not click an unknown link just because the message contains a plausible story. Verify the sender and access important services through their official website.

A 60-second checklist

  • Who first published the claim?
  • When was it published, and is the content current?
  • Does the evidence actually support the headline?
  • Can independent, reliable sources corroborate it?
  • Could an image, quote or statistic be out of context?
  • Would sharing it cause harm if it turns out to be wrong?
Best habit: When you cannot verify a claim, do not amplify it. “I don't know yet” is better than passing on misinformation.
